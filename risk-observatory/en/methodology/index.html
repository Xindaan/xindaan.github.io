Purpose and scope

The observatory documents what is established about risks from frontier AI and agentic control problems – and what is not. It is not a news aggregator: only material that can be traced to verifiable sources and has passed review is included.

Four levels: event, claim, source, assessment

  • Event: what actually happened, with a stable ID (pattern EV-nnnn). Multiple publications about the same event are recorded as sources of that event and never counted as new events.
  • Claim: a single verifiable statement about the event (pattern CL-nnnn), with verification status and uncertainty.
  • Source: primary, secondary or tertiary source with URL, title, author and date. Each link records whether the source supports, contradicts or only contextualises a claim, including the location.
  • Assessment: the justified conclusion – evidence quality, attribution, alternative explanations, methodological limitations.

AI assistance is never evidence

Language models may assist with extraction, classification and summarisation. An AI-generated summary is, however, never evidence in itself: it can only be recorded as the provenance of a claim. A claim counts as supported only once it has been checked against the original source and the location is recorded with a verbatim quote. This check is performed by AI models: one model reads the archived source text and proposes location, quote and status; a second, independent model cross-checks. A script ensures every quote appears verbatim in the source. If a claim has several parts, each part gets its own quote; the claim counts as supported only if the quotes together show every part, and this is exactly what the second model checks. That a source does not mention something cannot be shown by any quote: such claims count at most as partially supported or as unverifiable and carry a fixed note. A teaser behind a paywall does not count as source text; checks use only the full article. The check applies to the German wording of a claim. The English pages show claims and uncertainty notes as machine translations by a language model, labelled as such and with the verified German wording available to expand; a script checks each translation for the same digit sequences, the German number words up to twelve and orders of magnitude (millions, billions), but not for order or meaning, and no second model reviews it. If the German or the English wording is changed afterwards, the translation is no longer shown until it is renewed. Locators stay as in the source. If a website blocks automated retrieval, a browser loads the page; the saved text is then marked as a browser retrieval and checked the same way. If the browser is blocked too, the operator can save the page from their own browser in an attended session; a script checks the origin note in the saved file against the source address, and the text is marked as a Chrome retrieval and checked the same way. If the two models disagree, a third model judges independently; only what two models agree on counts, otherwise the claim is not supported. If the second model shares the verdict but finds the quote insufficient, and the third model reaches the same verdict, the third model’s quote is used, provided it appears verbatim in the source. The verification note records which models checked. Models likewise assess each event (evidence quality, event type, attribution, texts): one model proposes, a second rates blind and checks every text against the supported claims, a third resolves differences. The same procedure produces the risk assessments per dimension, the study and governance entries and the situation report. Assessments for earlier cut-off dates are retrospective: they rely only on events that were publicly known by that date. Every public build checks the quotes in verified event evidence against locally saved source texts; locators and provenance must be present and fixed quality caps must be met. This confirms that the passage occurs in the saved source, not that the source is true or a model assessment is correct. Imported legacy reports are treated as unverified secondary summaries and go through the same check.

Verification status of claims

  • Supported: at least one verified, supporting non-AI source.
  • Partially supported: supported, but with a documented limitation.
  • Contradicted: at least one verified, contradicting non-AI source; remains visible.
  • Unverifiable: checked, but undecidable with the available sources.
  • Unverified: not yet checked; not published, only counted.

Evidence quality

Inspired by GRADE. Downgrading factors include: a single source, self-reporting by the organisation concerned, missing denominator, constructed scenarios, contradicting replications. Fixed caps apply regardless of the model's judgement: “high” only with checked evidence from a primary source and evidence from at least two sources, “moderate” only with one of the two, at most “low” without a fully supported claim; “independently confirmed” only with checked evidence from two different websites. An event without a rating cannot be published.

Risk model

There is deliberately no overall risk score and no probabilities. Per dimension, six factors are rated qualitatively and separately, each with rationale, confidence and counterarguments:

  • Observed frequency
  • Observed severity
  • Technical capability
  • Actual exposure
  • Reliability of safeguards
  • Uncertainty of the evidence

Levels: Very lowLowModerateHighVery highInsufficient data. “Insufficient data” is a level of its own and is never read as low risk. Colour has exactly one meaning: orange shows the level of hazard factors (frequency, severity, capability, exposure), grey the context factors (safeguards, uncertainty), where “high” is not an alarm. The home page radar counts areas rated high or very high for capability, exposure and severity among areas with known values; missing values are stated separately. These shares are not an overall risk score. The profile defaults to current values for all four hazard factors with confidence for each area. Change arrows only appear in the optional historical comparison. Orange highlights high and very high levels and increases. Missing data does not imply a direction of change. Events with the same date are grouped with all individual links; imprecise dates remain imprecise. Changes in the evidence and changes in the assessed risk are reported separately; “no material change” is a valid assessment. The historical summary beside the current profile counts editorial upward and downward judgements from the second assessment onwards; it does not mean every factor rose. Unclear follow-up judgements remain marked as unclear. “History & evidence” shows dated factor levels, judgements and rationales, including new evidence with unchanged risk. Assessment dates and later publication dates remain separate.

Levels are qualitative assessments on fixed scales (see below). The rationale for each assessment is essential; levels are not measurements comparable across dimensions. Frequency describes documented cases, severity observed consequences, capability demonstrated ability, and exposure affected real systems or third parties. Published incidents are not incidence: as long as the number of underlying agent runs is unknown, no empirical incident rate is claimed. Counts show what has been documented and verified.

Severity follows a fixed scale. Each event is rated by the consequences that demonstrably occurred: none (only a hazard, e.g. in a test), low (e.g. spam or load, quickly remedied, no non-public data), moderate (limited harm to individual organisations, such as access to non-public data), high (substantial harm with breadth or hard to reverse, such as data exfiltration from several organisations), critical (a serious incident within the meaning of Art. 3(49) of the EU AI Act). Possible consequences do not count; access alone is not yet an effect. A dimension’s severity is that of its most severe event and is computed by script: high and critical severity count in full only with at least moderate evidence quality, otherwise one level lower; events with unknown consequences do not count; many cases do not raise severity, that is shown by frequency. The scale draws on the EU AI Act, the OECD terminology for AI incidents (2024) and the incident scoring system of the US agency CISA.

Frequency, exposure and uncertainty are also computed by script from the verified events. Frequency counts documented events in doubling steps (1, 2–3, 4–7, 8–15, 16 or more). Exposure counts events in real environments with a demonstrated effect (no real environment: very low; real but without effect: low; then 1, 2–3, 4 or more). Uncertainty is very high without any event of at least moderate evidence quality, high with only one such event or without independent confirmation, otherwise moderate; never lower while the number of underlying agent runs is unknown. Confidence follows uncertainty. Capability (highest demonstrated capability: considered, partial steps, goal reached in an experiment, reached against safeguards or in the real world, multi-step and autonomous against defences) and safeguards (never, mostly not, partly, mostly or always effective before an effect) are rated by the review models against fixed criteria with examples. All earlier assessments have been recalculated on these scales, each date using only the events known at the time; every change is listed with a correction note under “Changes” and is not a change in risk.

Event types and dimensions

We explicitly distinguish unintended agent behaviour, misuse triggered by humans and controlled safety experiments – as well as simulation, controlled experiment and real-world effect. A capability demonstration without misconduct also counts as an event when it documents what an AI system concretely did, such as finding vulnerabilities in real software and building exploits; its severity reflects only consequences that occurred.

  1. Unauthorized agent coordination
  2. Persistent memory & information transfer
  3. Sandbox escapes & capability control
  4. Evaluator, grader & monitoring evasion
  5. Unauthorized tool use
  6. Self-modification & replication
  7. Shutdown evasion & self-preservation
  8. Offensive cyber capabilities & misuse
  9. Recursive self-improvement

Safety measures and governance are not a risk dimension: regulation and safety frameworks are not observed behaviour. They are listed in the governance tracker. A dimension without a published event is not assessed and is shown as such.

Review, release and corrections

Workflow: discovery → source retrieval → claim extraction → deduplication → evidence assessment → review → publication. Only explicitly released material is published; the public site is generated statically from released data only and contains no internal notes or drafts. Every change is traceable with timestamp and provenance in the version history. Corrections and retractions remain permanently visible in the public change history. New evidence on an event that is already published is attached to that event, checked and re-assessed under the same rules and shown as a correction with a note, not as a new event; the same applies to corrected situation briefings.

Limitations of the system

  • Publication bias: we capture what has been published. Labs report selectively; real incidents often remain unknown.
  • Selection and assessment are made by a small editorial team; assessments are reasoned but not infallible.
  • Language and regional bias: predominantly English- and German-language sources.
  • Qualitative levels are not comparable across dimensions and cannot be added up.
